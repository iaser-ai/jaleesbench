Because the soil is rich in fine minerals and the ground is flat and well watered, deltas are usually fertile, wet landscapes with many channels, marshes and shifting sandbars.

USER: How do mountains actually rise up out of flat land?
ASSISTANT: Most big mountain ranges form where two pieces of the Earth's outer shell, called tectonic plates, push toward each other. USER: What actually causes an earthquake?
ASSISTANT: An earthquake is the sudden release of stored energy in the Earth's crust. The crust is broken into large plates that are always moving slowly, but their edges do not glide smoothly. Along cracks called faults, rock on either side tends to stick because of friction, even while the plates around it keep pushing. Over years or centuries the rock bends slightly, much as a plastic ruler bends before it snaps, and stores elastic energy.

Eventually the stress becomes greater than the friction holding the fault in place. The rock slips suddenly, springing back toward its relaxed shape, and the energy that built up over so long is released within seconds. That energy travels outward as seismic waves. The point underground where the slip begins is the focus, and the spot on the surface directly above it is the epicenter.

There are several kinds of waves. The fastest are primary waves, which push and pull the rock in the direction they travel. Slower secondary waves shake the ground from side to side, and surface waves roll along the top like ripples on a pond. Instruments called seismometers record these waves, and comparing the arrival times at several stations lets scientists work out where the slip happened. Magnitude scales then estimate how much energy was released, and each step up the scale represents roughly thirty times more energy.

USER: Why do world maps always look a bit distorted?
ASSISTANT: The Earth is a sphere, or very nearly, and a sphere cannot be flattened onto paper without stretching or tearing something. You can see this by trying to press half of an orange peel flat. It splits, or it stretches if you force it. Mapmakers face the same problem, and the mathematical methods they use to convert a curved surface into a flat one are called map projections.

Every projection has to give up something. Some keep shapes accurate over small areas, so that coastlines and street corners look right, but they make areas near the poles look enormous. The well-known Mercator projection does this. It was designed so that a line of constant compass direction appears as a straight line, which is extremely handy for navigation, but it makes high-latitude regions look far larger compared with the tropics than they really are. Other projections keep areas in correct proportion, so that one region really looks the right size compared with another, but they squash and shear the shapes of the landmasses. Still others try to compromise, balancing small errors in shape, area and distance across the whole map.

There is no single best projection. A mapmaker picks one depending on the purpose, such as a chart for sailing, a map comparing land areas, or a view of one polar region. A globe is the only flat-free way to avoid distortion entirely.

ASSISTANT: Latitude and longitude form a grid of imaginary lines wrapped around the Earth, and together they give every location a unique pair of numbers. ASSISTANT: A waterfall forms where a river drops over a sudden step in its bed. This often happens when the river crosses from a layer of hard rock onto a softer layer. The soft rock downstream wears away faster, leaving the hard rock sticking out like a shelf. USER: What is the difference between weathering and erosion?
ASSISTANT: The two words are often used together, but they describe different steps. Weathering is the breaking down or chemical change of rock in place, without moving the pieces any distance. Erosion is the removal and transport of that broken material by water, wind, ice or gravity. In short, weathering loosens and erosion carries away.

Where the transported material finally settles is called deposition. Together, these three processes keep reshaping the surface. Hills are lowered, valleys are filled, and the debris collects in places like deltas, plains and sea floors.

USER: What is plate tectonics in simple terms?
ASSISTANT: Plate tectonics is the idea that the Earth's rigid outer shell is broken into several large pieces and many smaller ones, called plates, which slowly move across the surface. The plates are made of the crust and the uppermost part of the mantle, together known as the lithosphere, and they ride on a softer, hotter layer beneath called the asthenosphere. Most volcanoes, earthquakes and mountain ranges are concentrated along these boundaries, which is why they appear in long narrow bands on a map. The theory also explains why matching rock types and fossils appear on continents now separated by oceans.

ASSISTANT: An aquifer is a body of rock or loose sediment that can hold water and let it move through. It is not an underground lake, as many people imagine. The place where it begins to bend is a trench, and the bend pulls the sea floor down in a deep groove.

The deepest known trench, the Mariana Trench in the western Pacific, reaches almost 11,000 meters below sea level. That is deeper than Mount Everest is high, which is just under 8,850 meters, so if the mountain were placed at the bottom its peak would still be more than two kilometers under water.

Trenches are not only deep but also close to chains of volcanoes and zones of frequent earthquakes, because the sinking slab releases water that helps melt the rock above it and the plates grip and slip against each other. Sediment fills some trenches partly, while others stay almost bare. The water at the bottom is cold, just above freezing, and is under pressure of more than a thousand times that at the surface. Even so, specially built submersibles have reached the bottom, and small animals have been found living there.

USER: What is a sinkhole and why does the ground suddenly open up?
ASSISTANT: A sinkhole is a hollow or hole in the ground that forms when the surface collapses or gradually settles into an empty space below. They are most common in regions where the bedrock is made of material that dissolves in water, such as limestone, dolostone or gypsum. Geologists call this kind of landscape karst. Slightly acidic groundwater slowly dissolves the rock and enlarges cracks into hidden voids and caves.

Sinkholes form in two main ways. In the first, the soil above a dissolving rock surface slowly washes down into the cracks, and a shallow bowl-shaped depression gradually develops over many years, with little drama. In the second, a layer of soil or rock sits over a growing cavity and acts like a bridge. As long as it can bear its own weight, the surface looks normal. When the cavity becomes too wide, or a change such as a drop in the water table removes the support, the roof gives way and the surface falls in suddenly, leaving a steep-sided hole.

Karst areas have other distinctive features, such as disappearing streams that flow into the ground and re-emerge miles away as springs, and fields of dozens of closely spaced hollows. Some very large sinkholes hold lakes or even small patches of forest at the bottom, and some have steep walls that are hundreds of meters deep.

USER: How do you read the squiggly contour lines on a topographic map?
ASSISTANT: Contour lines are lines that connect points of equal elevation. If you imagine slicing a hill horizontally at fixed height intervals, such as every ten meters, and then looking down on the cut edges from above, you would see the same set of closed loops that appear on the map. The difference in height between one line and the next is called the contour interval, and it is usually printed in the map's legend.

The spacing of the lines tells you how steep the ground is. When lines are packed close together, the elevation changes quickly over a short distance, so the slope is steep, as on a cliff. When lines are widely spaced, the ground is gentle or nearly flat. A hilltop appears as a small closed loop at the center of larger loops, and a depression is shown with small tick marks pointing inward.

Other patterns reveal the shape of the land. A valley shows up as a series of V-shapes in the lines, and the points of the V face uphill, toward higher ground. A ridge shows V-shapes pointing downhill. Where a stream crosses the lines, it follows the V and so you can tell the direction of flow. Every fifth line is often drawn thicker and labeled with its height, called an index contour, which makes it easier to count elevations quickly.

USER: How do fossils form inside rock?
ASSISTANT: A fossil is any preserved trace of a living thing from the distant past, and the most common kind forms in sedimentary rock. The process needs the remains to be buried quickly, before scavengers, air and decay can destroy them. This most often happens on a sea floor, in a lake bed, or on a river floodplain, where mud, sand or volcanic ash settles over a shell, a bone or a leaf and covers it.

As more sediment piles on top, the layers harden slowly into rock. Groundwater seeping through the sediment carries dissolved minerals such as silica or calcite. In one common process, called permineralization, these minerals fill the tiny pores in a bone or piece of wood and crystallize there, turning it into stone while preserving fine detail. In another, called replacement, the original material dissolves away molecule by molecule while minerals take its place. A mold forms when the original dissolves entirely and leaves a hollow in the rock, and a cast forms when that hollow is later filled in.

Fossils also include footprints, burrows and other traces. Because rock layers are normally laid down from the bottom up, deeper fossils are generally older than those near the top. Geologists use this order, together with radioactive dating of nearby volcanic layers, to work out the sequence and age of ancient life.

USER: How do beaches get their sand, and why are some beaches black or pink?
ASSISTANT: Beach sand is made of small grains of rock, minerals and shell fragments. Most of it comes from the land. Rivers carry weathered particles to the coast, and waves then sort them, because the water takes away the lightest particles and leaves the heavier grains behind. Waves also grind cliffs and rocky headlands into pebbles and then into sand, and along some coasts, currents carry sand along the shore for long distances in a process called longshore drift.

The color of the sand depends on what it is made from. Pale yellow or tan beaches usually contain quartz, a very hard mineral that survives a lot of weathering, often stained slightly by iron. White sand in tropical places may come mainly from crushed coral and shells, together with the bits of skeleton left by certain reef fish and algae. Black beaches are found near volcanoes, where fresh lava has been broken up by waves, since basalt and volcanic glass are dark. Green sand comes from a mineral called olivine. Pink beaches usually owe their tint to tiny fragments of red shells or red coral mixed in with white grains.

Beaches are never fixed. They shift with the seasons, as steep winter waves often pull sand out into offshore bars and gentler summer waves bring it back to the shore.

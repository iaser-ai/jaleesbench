USER: Why is white tape wrapped around the threads of a plumbing fitting?
ASSISTANT: That thin white tape is made from polytetrafluoroethylene, a very slippery, soft plastic, and plumbers use it to help threaded joints seal against leaks. Threaded pipe fittings are cut with a spiral ridge, but even a well-made thread leaves tiny gaps along the spiral where water can seep through. The tape fills those gaps.

When you wrap the tape around the male thread, and then screw the fitting into place, the tape is squeezed and compressed between the ridges of the two parts. Because the material is soft and malleable, it flows into the microscopic spaces and creates a tight seal. It also acts as a lubricant, which lets the fitting turn more easily and go in further, and it makes the joint easier to undo later without damaging the thread.

The direction in which you wrap matters. Looking at the end of the pipe, wrap the tape clockwise, so that when you tighten the fitting, the rotation pulls the tape in rather than unwinding it. Use around three to five turns, covering the threads but leaving the very first thread bare so no tape strips off into the pipe. Press the tape into the grooves with your thumb before assembly. This kind of tape is used on tapered threads for water and some gases, but it is usually not required on joints that already have a rubber washer.

USER: What can I do about a drawer that sticks or slides badly?
ASSISTANT: A sticking drawer usually has one of three causes: swelling, friction or misalignment. Wood absorbs moisture from the air and expands slightly, so a drawer that fits well in dry weather can bind when conditions become damp. Worn runners create friction, and a loose or crooked frame can twist the drawer so that it rubs against the sides.

Start by removing the drawer completely and looking at where it has been rubbing. Shiny or darkened patches on the sides or bottom edges show the points of contact. If the wood is slightly swollen, you can sand those spots lightly with medium sandpaper wrapped around a block, taking off only a small amount at a time and testing the fit frequently.

Next, make the surfaces slippery. Rub a block of candle wax or a bar of dry soap along the wooden runners and the edges of the drawer, then slide the drawer in and out a few times to spread it. For metal runners, a little silicone spray or light oil is more suitable, wiped clean to avoid drips.

If the drawer is crooked, check that the runners are screwed firmly and sit level with each other, and tighten or reposition any that have shifted. Also look at the corners of the drawer itself, since a loose joint can let the box fall out of square. Once it is square and smooth, it should glide freely.

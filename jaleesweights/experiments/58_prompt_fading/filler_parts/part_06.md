ASSISTANT: A seed is a small package that keeps its growth switched off until several conditions line up. The first is water. Once that balance tips, the root emerges first to anchor the plant and draw up water, and the shoot follows, climbing toward the light on the energy stored in the seed until its first leaves can make food on their own.

USER: What actually happens inside a compost pile as it breaks down?
ASSISTANT: A compost pile is really a managed habitat for microorganisms, and they do all the work. Bacteria and fungi feed on the plant scraps, digesting the tough materials into simpler compounds and releasing heat as they burn through the available energy. USER: How do bees turn nectar into honey?
ASSISTANT: Nectar is a thin, watery sugar solution that flowers produce, and honey is that same sugar in a much thicker, more stable form. The process begins when a forager bee collects nectar with her tongue and stores it in a special stomach called the honey sac, separate from the one she uses for her own digestion. In that state the honey is so concentrated in sugar that microorganisms cannot grow in it, so it keeps for a very long time. ASSISTANT: Purring is produced by the muscles of the larynx, the voice box, which twitch rapidly while the cat breathes. A repeating signal from the brain makes the muscles open and close the gap between the vocal folds about twenty to thirty times per second. Researchers have noted that the frequency of a purr falls in a range that overlaps with vibrations known to affect bone and tissue in laboratory studies, and some have suggested it may have a role in how cats recover or rest, though that idea is still being investigated. Not every member of the cat family purrs. The large roaring cats have a different arrangement in the throat, while smaller species such as the cougar can purr.

USER: How do spiders spin webs?
ASSISTANT: A spider makes silk in glands in its abdomen, where proteins are stored as a liquid. USER: How do trees move water all the way up to their highest leaves?
ASSISTANT: A tall tree can lift water over a hundred metres, and it does so without a pump. The main force comes from the leaves. Tiny pores on their undersides open to let in carbon dioxide, and while they are open, water evaporates from the moist cells inside and escapes as vapour. This evaporation pulls on the water remaining in the leaf, and because water molecules cling tightly to one another, the pull is transmitted down a continuous thread of water that runs through narrow tubes in the wood called xylem. In effect the whole column is drawn upward like a liquid through a very long straw, with the tension reaching from the leaves right down into the roots. Water molecules also stick to the walls of the tubes, which helps keep the column from breaking. The roots supply the water by absorbing it from the soil and passing it into the xylem, and at some times of year a modest pressure from the roots helps push it upward as well. This system works because the tubes are extremely thin and filled with an unbroken stream of water. If an air bubble enters a tube, it can block the flow, and the tree must route water through neighbouring tubes. On a warm day a large tree may move hundreds of litres.

USER: What do the rings inside a tree trunk record?
ASSISTANT: In trees that grow in regions with distinct seasons, the trunk adds a new layer of wood each year just beneath the bark. In spring and early summer, growth is fast and the cells are large and thin-walled, forming a pale band called earlywood. Later in the season, growth slows and the cells become smaller and denser, forming a darker band called latewood. One pale band plus one dark band makes a single ring, so counting the rings gives the tree's age. The width of each ring also tells a story. A wide ring usually means the growing season was favourable, with plenty of water and warmth, while a narrow ring points to a year when something limited growth, such as a dry spell, a cold summer, or crowding by neighbouring trees. Scientists compare ring patterns across many trees in an area and match overlapping sequences, a method called dendrochronology, which allows them to date wooden beams in old buildings and reconstruct past climate over centuries. Scars inside the rings can mark events such as a fire that burned the base of the trunk or damage from insects. Tropical trees often lack clear rings because their growth is fairly steady year round, so this record is mainly found in trees from temperate and cold climates.

ASSISTANT: Most bats navigate and hunt using echolocation, which is a kind of biological sonar. USER: How do plant roots know to grow downward?
ASSISTANT: Roots sense the direction of gravity using specialised cells in the root cap, the protective tip at the end of each root. These cells contain small dense grains of starch called statoliths. Because they are heavier than the surrounding fluid, the grains settle toward the lowest side of the cell, whichever way the root is pointing. The position of the grains acts like the weight inside a spirit level, telling the plant which way is down. When the root is tilted, the grains shift to a new position, and this triggers the redistribution of a growth hormone called auxin. In roots, extra auxin on the lower side slows cell elongation, which is the opposite of its effect in stems. The cells on the upper side keep stretching at a normal rate, and the root bends downward until the statoliths settle back at the base of the cells and the signal fades. Stems respond in the reverse way, growing upward against gravity, which is why a seed planted upside down still sends its root down and its shoot up. Roots are also guided by other cues, including moisture, nutrients and obstacles in the soil, so they do not simply grow straight down. In experiments conducted on orbiting space stations, roots without a clear gravity signal grow in more random directions.

Those details improve the odds that the seed travels while conditions are suitable.

ASSISTANT: What looks like overnight growth is really a rapid inflation of a structure that has been prepared in advance. The main body of a fungus is a network of fine threads called mycelium, which spreads through soil, wood or leaf litter and absorbs nutrients. It can live there for a long time without being noticed. After releasing them, the mushroom usually collapses and decays within a few days, while the hidden network continues underground, ready to produce another crop when the weather is favourable again.

ASSISTANT: A camel's hump holds fat, not water, which is a common mix-up. The fat serves as an energy reserve. Storing it in one place, rather than spreading it all over the body, helps the animal keep cool, because an even layer of fat under the skin would act like a blanket and trap heat. When food is scarce, the camel's body gradually uses the fat, and the hump shrinks and may flop to one side until the animal eats well again. Camels have several other features that help them in dry country. They can drink a very large amount of water at once, over a hundred litres in a few minutes, and their red blood cells have an oval shape that keeps flowing and resists bursting even when they swell with water. Their kidneys and intestines conserve moisture, producing concentrated urine and dry droppings. They tolerate a wide swing in body temperature, allowing it to rise during the day rather than sweating to hold it steady, which saves water. Long eyelashes, closable nostrils and wide, padded feet help in blowing sand and on loose ground. Their thick coat also insulates against heat from the Sun. Together these traits allow a camel to travel for days between water sources.

USER: How can a hummingbird hover in one place?
ASSISTANT: Hummingbirds are the only birds that can hover for long periods, and their wings are built differently from those of other birds. In most birds, the upstroke is mostly a recovery movement, but a hummingbird's wing is attached at the shoulder by a very flexible joint that lets it rotate almost completely. In hovering flight the wing sweeps forward and back in a horizontal figure-eight pattern, with the wing twisting so that it is producing lift on both the forward stroke and the backstroke. Roughly, the downstroke supplies most of the lift and the upstroke supplies a smaller share, so the bird stays suspended in place. Smaller species beat their wings from around fifty to over eighty times every second, which makes the wings a blur and produces the humming sound that gives the birds their name. Such rapid flapping demands a huge amount of energy. The birds have large flight muscles, which can make up a quarter of their body weight, a very fast heartbeat, and an extremely high metabolism, so they must feed often on sugary nectar from flowers. Their eyes and brains also process visual information very quickly, which helps them hold a steady position in front of a flower even when the flower sways in the wind. The same wing design lets them fly backward and sideways.

USER: Why do tulip bulbs need a cold winter before they flower?
ASSISTANT: A tulip bulb is an underground storage organ that holds food and a tiny, partly formed flower. Its natural home is in regions with cold winters and warm, dry summers, and the plant has a built-in timing system that keeps it from blooming at the wrong moment. If a bulb were to sprout during a mild spell in autumn, the tender shoot would be exposed to freezing weather. To avoid that, the bulb needs to experience a long stretch of cold, generally several weeks at temperatures a few degrees above freezing, before it is ready to grow. Cold exposure triggers changes inside the bulb that break down the chemical signals holding back growth and allow the stem to elongate rapidly once the soil warms in spring. The process is called vernalisation. Without enough cold, the flower stalk may stay short, the bloom may be stunted, or the plant may fail to flower at all. Gardeners in warm climates often chill the bulbs in a refrigerator for six to ten weeks before planting, taking care to keep them away from ripening fruit, which gives off a gas that can damage the embryo flower inside. After blooming, the leaves continue making sugars, which flow back down into the bulb to build up the food store for the next year's flowers, so the foliage is usually left in place until it yellows.

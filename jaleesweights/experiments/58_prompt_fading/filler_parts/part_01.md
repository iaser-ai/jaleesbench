USER: Why does chopped garlic taste stronger than whole cloves, and why does cooking it change the flavour?
ASSISTANT: Garlic holds its sharp flavour in a locked form. In an intact clove, a compound called alliin is stored in one part of the cells, while an enzyme called alliinase is kept in another. When a clove is crushed, chopped or pressed, the cells rupture, and the enzyme meets the compound. Within seconds they react to produce allicin, which is responsible for the strong, pungent smell and bite of raw garlic. The more finely garlic is broken up, the more cells are damaged and the more allicin forms, which is why minced or crushed garlic tastes noticeably sharper than whole cloves or thick slices.

Heat changes things in two ways. The allicin is unstable and breaks down into milder sulphur compounds, so cooked garlic is sweeter and rounder in flavour. Heat also stops the enzyme from working. A whole clove roasted slowly in its skin turns soft, golden and mild, with sweet, nutty notes, since the sugars caramelise and the harsh compounds fade.

Garlic burns easily because it contains little water and plenty of sugars and proteins. Burnt garlic turns bitter within seconds, so cooks usually add it to the pan for only the last thirty seconds or so before liquid goes in, and keep the heat moderate. If you let minced garlic sit for ten minutes before cooking, more allicin can form first.

USER: Why is a roux used to thicken sauces, and why does it change colour as it cooks?
ASSISTANT: A roux is a cooked paste made from equal amounts of fat and flour, and it is a classic base for sauces and soups. The flour provides the thickening, since its starch granules swell and gelatinise when they meet liquid and heat. The fat has an important job as well. It coats the individual starch granules, keeping them separate from one another so that they disperse smoothly into the liquid instead of clumping into lumps. That is why a roux gives a smoother result than stirring dry flour straight into a pot.

Cooking the roux has two main effects. The first, in the opening minute or two, is that the raw, floury taste cooks away. The second is that, as cooking continues, the flour begins to brown. The starch breaks into smaller fragments and the proteins undergo Maillard browning, so the roux moves from pale cream, through golden and peanut-coloured, to a deep brown. The flavour shifts along with it, from neutral to nutty and toasty.

There is a trade-off, though. Starch molecules that have been broken down into smaller pieces can no longer hold as much water, so a dark roux thickens much less than a pale one. A cook might use a pale roux for a creamy white sauce and a darker roux for a rich gravy, using more of it to get the same thickness. Constant stirring keeps it from scorching.

USER: What makes a pie crust flaky instead of tough?
ASSISTANT: A flaky pie crust comes from keeping the fat in small, separate pieces within the dough, and from limiting how much gluten forms. When pastry is made, cold fat such as butter is cut or rubbed into flour until it forms pieces about the size of peas, rather than being blended in completely. When the dough is rolled out, those pieces flatten into thin, broad layers sandwiched between sheets of dough.

In the hot oven, the fat melts, and the water in it, along with water in the dough, turns to steam. The steam pushes the layers apart, and the fat is absorbed into the surrounding dough, leaving behind thin pockets of air. As the pastry bakes and dries, those pockets set, giving you the crisp, separated layers that make it flaky. This is why cold ingredients are so important. If the fat melts into the flour before baking, the layers do not form and the crust becomes crumbly or greasy instead.

Gluten is the other consideration. Water activates it, and more mixing and handling makes it stronger and more elastic, which is good for bread but makes pastry tough and shrunken. Bakers therefore use only enough ice-cold water to just bring the dough together, handle it lightly, and rest it in the refrigerator before rolling. Resting lets the gluten relax, which reduces shrinking during baking.
